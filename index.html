About

I am a doctoral researcher in control engineering at the Faculty of Electrical Engineering, University of West Bohemia in Pilsen. My work sits between classical feedback control and learning-based methods, with an emphasis on what actually survives contact with real hardware.

Before Pilsen I completed a Master’s in Robotics at Nazarbayev University in Astana. There I designed a two-wheeled self-balancing mobile manipulator, trained it with curriculum reinforcement learning in NVIDIA Isaac Lab, and carried the resulting policies through sim-to-sim and sim-to-real transfer onto a physical robot running on ODrive controllers and a Jetson Orin NX.

Earlier work took me through EEG-based motor imagery classification for brain–computer interfaces, and through two years designing large-scale automated irrigation systems — hydraulics, pipeline sizing, pump selection and IoT telemetry. That stretch taught me how much of engineering is measurement and constraint rather than algorithm, and it is still how I approach a control problem.

Thesis — Reinforcement Learning Based Control System for a Two-Wheeled Self-Balancing & Path Planning Mobile Manipulator.

Thesis abstract

Reinforcement learning offers a route to autonomous control without explicit programming, but training on real hardware is slow and expensive, while simulation — for all its unlimited trials — opens a sim-to-real gap. This thesis develops a reinforcement learning framework that trains a custom two-wheeled self-balancing mobile manipulator to balance on its wheels and navigate to randomly generated target points. The robot was modelled in Fusion 360, Onshape and NVIDIA Isaac Sim so that mass, dimensions, torque, damping and velocity reflect the physical machine. Both velocity-based and torque-based control paradigms were trained under a curriculum learning strategy in Isaac Lab that decomposes the task into four stages: holding an upright balance, orienting toward the target, driving toward it, and performing a soft landing. The staged approach cut training time and dataset size while improving learning efficiency. Sim-to-sim and sim-to-real transfer experiments compared the trained policies against a PID controller; both paradigms achieved robust stabilisation and target-directed navigation, confirming that curriculum learning accelerates training and narrows the sim-to-real gap for complex robotic tasks.
